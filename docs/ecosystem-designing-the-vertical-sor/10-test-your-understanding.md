### Q1. Why does this page refuse to treat a profession's current workflow as the blueprint for its System of Record?

- **Most of it is accommodation to old limits** ✅
- It is always far too short to serve as a source
- It never contains any of the profession's real rules or controls
- Regulators forbid reusing old workflows

**Explanation:** The page argues most pieces of a current workflow are not the profession at all: they are accommodations that work around the limits of the people and machines that did the work, so copying them carries those limits forward. Length is never the objection, and a short workflow could still be full of truths. The old workflow certainly does contain real rules, which is exactly why the method studies it through archaeology instead of discarding it. No regulator forbids reusing a documented workflow, and Bin 1 exists precisely because some of it must be kept. Carry the truths, and leave the museum of old steps behind.
*(Source: Section: Why the old workflow is the wrong blueprint)*

### Q3. A SaaS application is a system of record, a set of capabilities, and a workflow UI. Which part dies first in the agentic era, and why?

- The system of record, because agents keep their own memory
- **The workflow UI, because its purpose was the human** ✅
- The capabilities, because agents replace every function
- All three at once, as agents rebuild apps

**Explanation:** The workflow UI exists only so a human can drive the capabilities by hand, so when an agent operates the capabilities instead, the UI dies first. The system of record does not die; it is the part you carry forward, and building it well is this page's entire subject. The capabilities survive too, because the agent operates them. Nothing suggests all three collapse together. The same decomposition applies to a profession: its steps and screens were the workflow UI of the human-only era, while the knowledge, the evidence, and the judgment are the record.
*(Source: Section: Why the old workflow is the wrong blueprint)*

### Q4. What distinguishes first principles thinking from thinking by copying?

- It skips any study of how the work is done today
- It is faster than copying in nearly every case
- **It builds up from basic truths, not from imitation** ✅
- It always produces a solution that nobody has ever attempted before

**Explanation:** First principles thinking breaks a problem down to the truths that remain when every habit and convention is removed, then builds up from those truths alone, while copying imitates what others already do and quietly imports their limits. It is not faster; copying is the fast path, which is exactly why it tempts. It does not skip studying the current work, and this page adds workflow archaeology for that reason, because the truths hide inside the old work. Novelty is a side effect, not the definition. The cook and chef contrast makes it concrete: a cook follows recipes, and a chef understands the ingredients themselves.
*(Source: Section: First principles, applied asymmetrically)*

### Q5. Why does the page forbid applying first principles to the external corpus?

- Because external sources are usually far too long for anyone to rewrite
- Because a rewrite would breach every publisher's license
- Because agents cannot read law and standards reliably
- **Because those rules are already the jurisdiction's first principles** ✅

**Explanation:** Laws and standards say what they say, and those external rules are themselves the first principles of your jurisdiction: the truths that remain when every habit is removed. Your job with them is faithful service, keeping each source complete, versioned, and citable. Length is irrelevant to the argument. Licensing is a real concern, handled by the rights basis and the register, but it is not why first principles must not be applied. Agents read law perfectly well when it is governed and citable, which is the whole point of a corpus. A founder who rethinks the tax code has built a system that gives wrong answers confidently.
*(Source: Section: First principles, applied asymmetrically)*

### Q6. Why must reflexes be derived from scratch rather than converted from existing procedures?

- Because existing procedures sit in unreadable file formats
- Because the regulator requires new wording each year
- Because a reflex must always be shorter than the procedure it replaces

**Explanation:** All existing procedures, checklists, and templates were written for human workers, so all of them carry human workarounds. A reflex must be written fresh for a Worker that finds the right corpus sections, loads the complete procedure, performs every required check, and never gets tired at file forty. File formats are irrelevant, and converting an SOP to Markdown is named as failure mode two precisely because the format changed while the procedure did not. No regulator requires annual rewording. Reflexes do need to fit in working attention, but that is a design constraint, not the reason for fresh derivation.

### Q14. What single question does the three-bin sort ask about every element of the old workflow?

- How long does this element take to complete
- Who owns this element today
- **Why does this element exist at all** ✅
- How often is this element performed each month

**Explanation:** The sort asks one question of every checklist item, form, handoff, report, and sign-off: why does this exist? There are only three honest answers, and each answer is a bin. Duration matters for the baseline number, not for the sort. Ownership tells you the org chart, which the method does not copy. Frequency helps you choose a first Worker, but it cannot tell you whether an element is law, a human workaround, or a technology workaround. One element means one instruction a person could follow or skip on its own, so a checklist line hiding three actions is split into three before it is sorted.

### Q15. A regulator names a separation of duties your workflow enforces. Which bin does it belong in?

- It stays unsorted until a customer decides
- Bin 2: redesign it, because a Worker holds the whole file
- Bin 3: delete it, because modern systems make it needless
- **Bin 1: keep it, because law and trust require it** ✅

**Explanation:** Bin 1 holds what law and trust require: the partner's signature, human approval before money moves, the separation of duties the regulator names, and the evidence a standard demands. These are not overhead, and deleting them is not innovation, because in a profession that sells trust the named human judgment is the product. Bin 2 covers elements that exist because humans forget or tire, which is not why a regulator writes a control. Bin 3 covers pre-AI system limitations. Leaving elements unsorted defeats the method, and the sort record is how you defend the design in a compliance meeting.
*(Source: Section: The three-bin sort)*

### Q16. The tick-and-tie checklist exists because a tired human misses items. What does the sort do with it?

- Keeps it unchanged, because completeness is required
- Deletes it, because complete checking is no longer needed at all
- Moves it into the customer's own private instance

**Explanation:** Bin 2 elements exist because of human limits, and they are never simply deleted: the purpose survives and is rebuilt as Worker capability plus a checker the reviewer can trust. Here the Worker checks everything and produces an exception report, so the reviewer reads five exceptions instead of ticking four hundred lines. Deleting it loses completeness, which is a genuine purpose. Keeping it unchanged preserves a workaround for a limit the Worker does not have. Moving it to the customer instance concerns customer-private knowledge, which is a different boundary entirely.

### Q17. 'A manager reviews every transaction.' How does the sort's sharpest refinement handle this control?

- The whole control is Bin 1 and stays exactly as written
- **The purpose is Bin 1; the review-everything mechanism is Bin 2** ✅
- The whole control is Bin 2 and disappears completely
- The control is Bin 3, because the systems now talk to each other

**Explanation:** A control's purpose is Bin 1; its mechanism is usually Bin 2. Here the purpose, that risk stays controlled, is an invariant and is kept, while reviewing everything is a human-limits workaround: routine cases pass automated checks, and the manager's attention goes to the exceptions. Keeping the whole control unchanged preserves a mechanism built for a limit that no longer applies. Treating the whole control as Bin 2 would delete a real invariant. Bin 3 covers retyping and reconciliation between systems that could not talk. The risk is still controlled, and the attention is spent exactly where it matters.
*(Source: Section: The three-bin sort)*

### Q18. Which of these belongs in Bin 3?

- A checklist that catches items a tired reviewer misses
- **Typing figures again from one system into another one** ✅
- The retention period a regulator's circular specifies

**Explanation:** Bin 3 is what old technology required: retyping figures between systems, renaming files, reformatting the same numbers for three reports, and status emails that exist only to make information visible. Nothing survives from this bin as itself. The partner's sign-off is Bin 1, since the standards assign that judgment to a named human. The tired-reviewer checklist is Bin 2, where the purpose returns as capability plus a checker. A regulator's retention period is Bin 1, and the page warns that such rules often look like habits. If a Bin 3 element hides a real purpose, it was sorted wrong: move the purpose up first, then delete the mechanism.

### Q21. What separates an invariant from ordinary guidance?

- An invariant appears more often in the procedure
- **Violating it makes the result invalid or untrustworthy** ✅
- An invariant is longer and more detailed than guidance is
- An invariant applies only to the first Worker you deploy

**Explanation:** A rule that is merely useful is guidance, and a rule whose violation makes the result invalid, unlawful, or untrustworthy is an invariant, which is why the page insists the list stays short. Frequency in the written procedure proves nothing, since the most important rules are often assumed rather than repeated. Length is irrelevant, and good invariants are usually short sentences like 'the preparer of a payment cannot approve it'. Invariants are not scoped to one Worker: they stay true even when the workflow, the software, the organization, and the customer all change.
*(Source: Section: The three-bin sort)*

### Q22. What does the page mean when it says 'relevance is not enough'?

- A retrieved passage must be short enough to quote
- A passage must be approved by the customer before it may be used
- **A passage must be applicable: right rung, jurisdiction, version** ✅
- A passage must appear in at least two separate sources

**Explanation:** Without a register entry, a system can retrieve a perfectly relevant passage from the wrong jurisdiction, the wrong version, or a retired standard, so the source must also be applicable. Length is a formatting question with no bearing on authority. Customer approval governs customer-specific policy, which is one rung among several, not a gate on every retrieval. Appearing in two sources is corroboration rather than applicability, and a rule can be perfectly applicable while stated only once. The register is governance, not paperwork: publisher, class and scope, jurisdiction, version, effective period, rights basis, owner, and a stable ID.
*(Source: Section: The source hierarchy: which authority wins)*

### Q23. How should a vertical handle a second country's rules?

- Blend both countries' rules into one combined page
- Start a completely separate System of Record for each new country you serve
- Apply the first country's rules until a regulator objects
- **Add each country as its own ladders in the same System of Record** ✅

**Explanation:** Jurisdiction is a scope on every authority entry, the Worker resolves the case's jurisdiction before it retrieves, and each new country is added as its own set of ladders inside the same System of Record, sharing the expert's methodology rungs where the profession is the same. Blending two countries into one page is explicitly forbidden, because a blended page cannot be cited in either country. A wholly separate System of Record throws away the shared methodology that makes the vertical reusable. Applying the wrong country's rules until someone objects is exactly the failure the scope rule prevents. And a new country is a new build whose coverage starts at one outcome again, not a thicker version of what you had.
*(Source: Section: The source hierarchy: which authority wins)*

### Q24. Content belongs in the authority class if it passes at least one of three tests. Which set is correct?

- **The citation test, the change test, and the dispute test** ✅
- The length test, the clarity test, and the recency test
- The owner test, the license test, and the version test
- The retrieval test, the ranking test, and the accuracy test

**Explanation:** The three tests are citation (will a Worker ever need to cite this to a reviewer), change (can it be different next year or in the next jurisdiction), and dispute (could two professionals disagree, so that authority must settle the question). Length, clarity, and recency are style and freshness concerns rather than authority. Owner, license, and version are columns in the register that authority content receives once it qualifies, not the qualifying tests themselves. Retrieval and ranking are properties of the search layer. Tax rates pass the change test, and classification judgments pass the dispute test.
*(Source: Section: Two kinds of content: authority and orientation)*

### Q25. Orientation fails all three authority tests and still belongs in the corpus. What is its own test?

- **Would a professional reader be lost without it** ✅

Retrieval frequency is the opposite of the goal, since orientation is marked as context precisely so it does not crowd out the rules. ### Q29. Every page in the System of Record is held to three bars at once. Which set is correct?

- Short, fully translated, and reviewed once every single quarter
- **Simple to read, exact to check, structured to cite** ✅
- Original, comprehensive, and free of external citations
- Searchable, printable, and available everywhere

**Explanation:** The three bars are simple enough for the day-one junior to read, exact enough for the reviewer to check, and structured enough for the Worker to cite. Readability flows one way: text a beginner can read, an expert reads faster and an agent parses more reliably. Exactness is the agent's bar and sits higher than the beginner's, because 'big entries need extra approval' is understandable and useless while 'entries above $500,000 (Firm Policy 4.2, version 2026-03)' is both simple and exact. Structure is invisible to every human reader. Translation, printing, and locale coverage are delivery concerns, not bars.

### Q34. A builder sees five departments in the invoice process and designs five agents. What has gone wrong?

- Five agents is too many for one outcome to support
- Each department should really get two agents instead
- **The org chart was copied, so the handoffs survive** ✅
- The agents cannot share a single governed corpus

**Explanation:** Designing one agent per department copies the org chart without improving the work: the customer never valued the handoffs, only the completed outcome. The count is not the objection, and the decision map for invoice review names eight decisions. Doubling the agents per department makes the copying worse. Sharing a corpus is not the problem either, since all of them could read the same one while the handoffs remained. Map the decisions the outcome requires instead, because decisions last longer than any department and a customer can assign them to different teams while the professional logic stays reusable.
*(Source: Section: Map decisions, not departments)*

### Q35. What separates a rule from a judgment in this method?

- A rule can be escalated, whereas a judgment cannot be
- A rule is written by the expert and a judgment by the regulator
- A rule applies to money and a judgment applies to documents
- **A rule has a clear condition and a clear result to apply** ✅

**Explanation:** Rules have a clear condition and a clear result, such as 'over the limit needs approval' or 'a matching number and amount means a possible duplicate', and they become automatic checks. Judgment requires interpretation, like whether evidence is sufficient or an exception is material, and it is supported with authoritative sources, expert guidance, examples and counterexamples, stated uncertainty, and an escalation boundary. Authorship does not distinguish them, since both can come from either source. Subject matter does not either. Judgments are exactly what gets escalated, so that option inverts the truth. Do not hide judgment inside a long prompt and call it a rule.
*(Source: Section: Rules, judgment, and permissions are three different things)*

### Q36. Why are 'prepare a transaction' and 'execute an irreversible action' listed as separate permission grants?

- **Because a Worker that prepares a payment must not release it** ✅
- Because preparing is slower than executing in practice
- Because they are performed by different software systems
- Because regulators require every single action to be logged twice over

**Explanation:** Permissions decide what a Worker may do after reaching a conclusion, and read, search, calculate, draft, recommend, prepare, reversible execution, and irreversible execution are separate grants, because a Worker that prepares a payment must not automatically release it. Which software systems are involved is irrelevant to the boundary. Relative speed has nothing to do with it. Double logging is not the reason, though the retained record does matter. In regulated domains the safe starting boundary is preparation and recommendation, with a named human approving and acting, and greater authority is earned through evaluations, never assumed.
*(Source: Section: Rules, judgment, and permissions are three different things)*

### Q37. How does the page say a high-risk rule should be enforced?

- By repeating it in the system prompt several times over
- **By tool permissions, approval gates, and policy checks** ✅
- By training the expert to review every output
- By writing it in bold at the top of the relevant reflex

**Explanation:** High-risk rules need more than words, so they are also enforced through tool permissions, approval gates, and policy checks, and the rule then holds even on the Worker's worst day. Repeating a rule in a system prompt is failure mode four, where important controls exist only as sentences that are unversioned, untested, and unenforced. Having the expert review every output does not scale and wastes the scarcest resource in the build. Bold text is formatting, and the two-classes section already showed that formatting is a visible mark rather than a control. The definition of done makes this its own checkbox.
*(Source: Section: Rules, judgment, and permissions are three different things)*

### Q38. Why does the method design the exceptions before finishing the normal path?

- Because exceptions are more common than routine cases
- Because routine cases need no written procedure at all
- **Because exceptions decide whether the Worker can be trusted** ✅
- Because the evaluation set must be written before the reflex

**Explanation:** Routine cases are easy to demonstrate, but exceptions decide whether the Worker can be trusted, so the failure shapes are listed before the standard reflex is finished: missing evidence, conflicting evidence, outdated sources, jurisdiction uncertainty, low confidence, tool failures, impossible deadlines, and customer instructions that conflict with professional rules. Exceptions are not more common than routine work; they are simply where trust is built. Routine cases very much need a procedure, which is what the reflex is. The evaluation set matters, but it exists to prove the slice. Failure mode six names the alternative: the happy-path demo.
*(Source: Section: Design the exceptions before the normal path)*

### Q39. What is the bar a useful escalation must clear?

- It must be sent within one hour of first detection
- It must contain the Worker's numeric confidence score
- It must be approved by a second Worker before sending
- **It must reduce the human's work, even when unfinished** ✅

**Explanation:** The bar is simple: an escalation must reduce the human's work, even when the Worker cannot finish. A useless escalation says only that it is unable to continue, handing the whole case back to start from zero. A useful one names the blocker, confirms what is already verified, states the one open requirement, and asks for the specific approval needed, so the checks already done stay done and the human receives a decision ready to make. Timing is not the standard, and a fast but empty escalation still fails. A confidence score is not the same as stated reasoning. A second Worker approving is not part of the design.
*(Source: Section: Design the exceptions before the normal path)*

### Q40. The old Friday review existed because human eyes were scarce. What question should replace it?

- **Whether the Worker should act on arrival, threshold, or deadline** ✅
- Whether the batch should run daily rather than weekly
- Whether the review should move to some other department entirely
- Whether more reviewers should be assigned to each batch

**Explanation:** Instead of inheriting the batch, ask whether the Worker should act when the document arrives, when a threshold is crossed, or when a deadline comes near, and keep a batch only where a real deadline requires it. Moving from weekly to daily keeps the batch assumption and merely tightens it. Moving the review to another department copies the org chart, which the decision map already rejected. Adding reviewers scales the human workaround instead of removing it. The second fresh question is sequence: the old order of steps often followed the org chart, and the new order should follow evidence and risk.
*(Source: Section: Rebuild the reflexes)*

### Q41. Your expert says a file takes four hours in her practice. Is that the baseline for a contract of success?

- Yes, because the expert is the authority on the profession
- **No: it is what you design against; the baseline is the customer's own number** ✅
- Yes, provided the expert's firm is similar in size to the buyer's
- No: baselines are only ever measured after the Worker is deployed

**Explanation:** Two things are easy to confuse. Your expert's practice told you the shape of the work and roughly what it costs, and that is what you designed against. The baseline in a signed contract is your customer's, measured in his own workflow after the sponsor conversation, because that is the only number the buyer can verify. Design against the expert's world, and contract against the customer's. Firm size does not transfer the number, since the buyer still cannot verify someone else's practice. And baselines are measured before deployment, not after: without a starting point agreed up front, success is undefined.
*(Source: Section: Rebuild the reflexes)*

### Q42. What does the page say a first build should contain?

- Ten procedures covering the profession's most common work
- Every source the profession uses anywhere, fully loaded and indexed
- **One complete slice: outcome, reflex, checker, evaluation set** ✅
- A working demo of the three most impressive use cases

**Explanation:** Build one complete, trusted slice: one outcome, its outcome contract, its part of the source hierarchy, one map, one fully derived reflex, one permission list, one exception list, one checker, one evaluation set, and one governance process. Ten incomplete procedures prove nothing, while one procedure that survives real professional review proves the architecture. Loading every source is the document dump, failure mode one. A demo of impressive use cases is failure mode six, and a polished demo is not proof. The evaluation set must include incomplete cases, conflicting evidence, wrong-jurisdiction sources, escalations, forbidden requests, and cases where the honest answer is that the evidence is not enough.
*(Source: Section: Build one thin slice, and prove it)*

### Q43. Thin and thick describe a System of Record. What do the two words count?

- **Outcomes, and every outcome present is covered completely** ✅
- How polished the prose is, from draft to final
- The number of sources loaded into the corpus
- How much of the expert's total knowledge has been captured

**Explanation:** Thin and thick count outcomes, never corners: one outcome is thin, many is thick, and every outcome present is covered completely whichever state you are in, so governance starts in the first week rather than later. Polish is not the axis, and thin is not a draft or a prototype. Source count is not the axis either, because one outcome can pull in an entire standard and three regulator interpretations, since size is whatever the decisions require. Total knowledge captured is closer to the twin's curriculum than to coverage. A slice that handles only the clean cases is not thin: it is unfinished, and it will not survive a professional's first hard question.
*(Source: Section: Build one thin slice, and prove it)*

### Q44. Why must the first slice be finished work rather than a rough cut you tidy up later?

- Because a regulator inspects it before the launch decision
- Because the retrieval layer rejects incomplete documents
- **Because it is the only credential you carry into the sponsor meeting** ✅
- Because the expert twin cannot be built from unfinished content

**Explanation:** This one slice is what you carry into the sponsor conversation that gate 3 asks for. A buyer reading one governed page of his own profession, plain words at the top and his own rules cited below, is not sitting through a pitch, so the slice is finished work from day one: it is the only credential you have. No regulator inspects the validation gates, which are the founder's own discipline. The retrieval layer indexes whatever it is given, which is exactly why governance is the control rather than the technology. The twin does stand on the System of Record, but that dependency comes after launch, not before the first meeting.
*(Source: Section: Build one thin slice, and prove it)*

### Q45. Why must every change to the corpus carry an impact record?

- Because publishers require notice of updates
- Because the retrieval index rebuilds only when changes are logged
- Because reviewers are paid according to the number of changes
- **Because a reflex can still apply last year's rule** ✅

**Explanation:** Every change gets an impact record stating what changed, why, who approved it, and which maps, reflexes, and evaluation cases are affected, because without that chain the corpus can be current while a reflex quietly applies last year's rule. Publisher notice is a licensing matter handled by the rights basis. Reviewer pay is irrelevant. Index rebuilding is a retrieval-layer mechanism that does not depend on a governance record. Governance is built in the same week as the content, never later: every source, map, and reflex gets an owner, a review, an approval state, and a version.
*(Source: Section: Build one thin slice, and prove it)*

### Q46. Which of these makes a System of Record bigger without making it thicker?

- A second outcome, derived with the expert and fully covered
- **Method pages copied in from the Agent Factory System of Record** ✅
- A new source the decision map actually cites
- An exception entry for a failure shape you had missed

**Explanation:** Three additions add weight without coverage: method content copied in from the Agent Factory System of Record, which adds retrieval noise and a second version to maintain; customer content moved up from a Layer 4 instance, which is contamination; and orientation grown into a full course, which is the unmarked textbook. Only a completed outcome thickens the System of Record. A second fully covered outcome is exactly what thickening means. A source the decision map cites is authority pulled by a decision, which is how the corpus is supposed to grow. A missed exception entry completes an outcome rather than padding it.
*(Source: Section: Build one thin slice, and prove it)*

### Q47. What does the coverage register record, and what is the point of it?

- Every source in the corpus, so licences can be renewed on time
- Every Worker deployed, so customers can be invoiced correctly
- **Every outcome and jurisdiction, so coverage is a fact not a feeling** ✅
- Every sorting decision, so the design can be defended later

**Explanation:** Template 7, the coverage register, holds one row per professional outcome per jurisdiction, with its state, the date it passed the definition of done, the expert's approval, and its next re-check, so you can answer 'how much of the profession do you cover?' with a fact instead of a feeling. Sources belong to Template 4, the source register. Sorting decisions belong to Template 2, the sort record. Deployed Workers and invoicing are engagement matters, not corpus governance. An outcome enters the coverage register only once it has passed the definition of done, because there is no partial credit: a half-covered outcome is unfinished, not thin.
*(Source: Section: The templates)*

### Q48. The definition of done is described as two lists in one. What is the second one?

- **Your definition of ready to sell** ✅
- Your definition of ready to hire
- Your definition of ready to promote into the platform
- Your definition of ready to add a second jurisdiction

**Explanation:** The list is the definition of done for one outcome, and it is also your definition of ready to sell, because the last unchecked box is the reason you are not yet in a buyer's meeting. Nothing on it can be deferred until after the first customer: the governance, the exception cases, and the expert's approval are exactly what make the slice worth showing. Hiring is not what the list governs. Promotion into a shared platform layer is the model's promotion law, with its own separate conditions. Adding a jurisdiction is a new build whose own coverage starts at one outcome again, not something this checklist gates.
*(Source: Section: The templates)*

### Q49. Failure mode seven is called first-principles theater. What does it look like in practice?

- **Deleting a Bin 1 element that turned out to be the law** ✅
- Refusing to change any part of the old workflow at all, ever
- Writing the invariants before studying the old cases
- Publishing the corpus before the expert has approved it

**Explanation:** First-principles theater is the boldest failure: deleting Bin 1 because it looked like an old habit, then discovering in the customer's compliance meeting that it was the law. The cure is the sort's first warning, that when in doubt an element stays in Bin 1 until the governing sources and the expert confirm its purpose can be safely redesigned. Refusing to change anything is the opposite error, the AI-readable SOP. Writing invariants early is not a failure, provided they are sourced and approved. Publishing without expert approval breaks the definition of done, but it is a governance lapse, not theater.
*(Source: Section: The failure modes)*

### Q50. A team loads hundreds of files into a vector database with semantic search and calls it a System of Record. Which failure mode is this?

- Technology first, cured by starting from the outcome
- **The document dump, cured by the source hierarchy and register** ✅
- The happy-path demo, cured by designing exceptions first
- Authority before evidence, cured by proving one thin slice first

**Explanation:** Hundreds of files plus semantic search, with no hierarchy, owners, or versions, is the document dump: searchable content, not a System of Record, and the cure is the source hierarchy with its register of publisher, class and scope, jurisdiction, version, and rights basis. Technology first is choosing a vector database and an agent framework before the outcome and the invariants exist, a related but separate error. The happy-path demo is testing only clean examples. Authority before evidence is letting a Worker act before its knowledge has passed evaluations. All four are named separately because each needs a different cure.
*(Source: Section: The failure modes)*

### Q51. Ayesha sorts three levels of review in her aunt's firm: junior prepares, senior reviews, manager reviews. How does the sort handle them?

- All three are Bin 1, because review protects quality
- All three are Bin 2, because the Worker holds the whole file
- **Each level is sorted separately, by its own purpose alone** ✅
- All three are Bin 3, because the systems now talk to each other

**Explanation:** The three levels split across the bins, and Ayesha sorts each one by its purpose: review that the standards or the firm's quality policy require is Bin 1 and stays, while review that existed to catch arithmetic, missing references, and incomplete files is Bin 2, work the Worker and the checker now do completely. Only after that split does she know which human reviews remain. Treating all three as Bin 1 preserves mechanisms built for human limits. Treating all three as Bin 2 would delete a required control. Bin 3 covers retyping and system reconciliation, not review. The partner's going-concern sign-off, meanwhile, is Bin 1 untouched.
*(Source: Section: Ayesha sorts her aunt's checklist)*

### Q52. Ayesha's aunt's files show four hours per working paper. What is that number, at this stage?

- The baseline in a contract of success, ready to sign
- The target the Worker must beat in production
- **What she designs against, until a customer supplies his own number** ✅
- An invented number, which Test 5 forbids

**Explanation:** Four hours is her aunt's number, from her aunt's practice, and it is what Ayesha designs against. It is not a target either; forty minutes is the target. ### Q53. In the sales appendix, what is the boundary between the CRM and the Sales System of Record?

- The CRM becomes the corpus once all of its data has been indexed
- The CRM is replaced entirely by the System of Record
- The System of Record stores the deals the CRM cannot hold
- **The CRM holds deals; the System of Record holds the profession** ✅

**Explanation:** The CRM stays what it is, the database of accounts, contacts, and opportunities, while the System of Record governs something the CRM never held: what counts as qualification evidence, which claims may reach a buyer, when a stage may advance, and what only a human may decide. The CRM is not replaced, and the model's competitive answer is that it extends existing systems. The System of Record does not store deals. Indexing CRM data does not make it a corpus, and the Worker treats existing CRM fields as claims to be checked, never as facts to be repeated. The ledger appendix draws the same line with the ERP.

### Q54. Why does every important field in the sales deal file carry an evidence state?

- **So no field looks more certain than the evidence behind it** ✅
- So the CRM can sort opportunities by data completeness
- So the forecast can be produced without any human review at all
- So the rep can be scored on their data-entry discipline

**Explanation:** Every important field carries one of six states, confirmed, indicated, inferred, unknown, contradicted, or outdated, so that no CRM field looks more certain than the evidence behind it, and an assumption written as a confirmed fact is the sales version of an invented citation. Sorting by completeness is a reporting nicety, not the purpose. Removing human review from the forecast contradicts the appendix, where the account executive owns every deal decision. Scoring the rep on data entry recreates the discipline the Worker exists to make unnecessary. The end-to-end case shows the states working: problem confirmed, sponsor indicated, budget unknown, next step contradicted.

### Q55. The sales evaluation set needs one case family the routine tests miss. What is it?

- Buyers who ask all of their questions in an unfamiliar language
- **Pressure from the seller's own side to inflate the pipeline** ✅
- Deals larger than the vertical has previously handled
- Competitors publishing misleading claims about the product

**Explanation:** The missing family is pressure from the inside: the seller asks the Worker to advance a stage without evidence, invent urgency, hide an objection, or mark a deal qualified so the forecast looks better. In every one of those cases the passing answer is a refusal with the evidence stated, because a sales Worker that cannot resist its own team's optimism will quietly fill the pipeline with deals that are not real. Language, deal size, and competitor claims are real situations, but none of them tests whether the invariants hold against the people the Worker works for. The ledger appendix has the same shape with an executive's unsupported entry.

### Q56. Accounting has one ladder per question rather than one ladder overall. What must the Worker do first?

- Retrieve every available source and rank the results by relevance
- Apply tax law first, then check the reporting framework
- **Identify the question, then use the ladder for that question** ✅
- Ask the controller which framework applies to this case

**Explanation:** The Worker first identifies the question, financial reporting, tax, or something else, then uses the ladder for that question, and tax law does not outrank US GAAP or IFRS inside the financial statements because it governs a different question entirely. Retrieving everything and ranking by relevance is exactly the mistake the register prevents, since relevance is not applicability. Applying tax law first inverts the ladders. Asking the controller every time would waste the human's attention on something the hierarchy already answers, and in this design the controller's job is judgment and sign-off, not routing.

### Q57. The month-end batch splits across bins in the accounting appendix. How?

- Neither part can be sorted until the close is automated
- The deadline is Bin 2 and the work itself is Bin 1
- Both the deadline and the work are Bin 3
- **The deadline is Bin 1; batching the work is Bin 2** ✅

**Explanation:** The reporting deadline is Bin 1 because the period is a legal reality, while batching the work into month-end is Bin 2: the work spreads across the month and the deadline is met early. This is the appendix's best lesson, that one old element can split across bins, and the pile of work behind a deadline does not inherit the deadline's authority. Reversing the two would delete a legal reality while preserving a human workaround. Bin 3 covers retyping sub-ledger totals and printing statements, which are separate rows in the same table. Waiting for automation before sorting inverts the method, since the sort is what tells you what to build.
*(Source: Section: Appendix B: A General Ledger System of Record, end to end)*

### Q58. A project manager asks for a $25,000 June consulting accrual. The Worker prepares $24,000. Why?

- **Because the evidence gives a stronger measurement basis** ✅
- Because the request exceeded the Worker's approval limit
- Because a rounding policy requires whole thousands
- Because the prior month's accrual set the same amount

**Explanation:** The statement of work exists, the approved rate is $150 per hour, and the project manager's own confirmation shows 160 hours completed in June, so the Worker prepares 160 times $150, because the evidence provides a stronger measurement basis than the request. The calculation, the evidence, and the difference from the requested amount all go into the file, and the approver, who is not the preparer, signs a number the evidence chose. No rounding policy is involved. Approval limits govern what a Worker may do, not what the number should be. No prior accrual covers the work, which the Worker checked. The Worker serves the books, not the request.

### Q59. Sales and the general ledger produce very different Workers from the same method. What stays identical?

- The depth of the source hierarchy in each of the domains
- **The shared spine: outcome, sort, and honest proof** ✅
- The permissions granted to the Worker at launch
- The kind of corruption each domain is prone to
