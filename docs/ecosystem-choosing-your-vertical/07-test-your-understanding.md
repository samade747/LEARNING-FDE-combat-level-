### Q1 — Choosing Your Vertical (intro)

**Q:** A graduate has a rich governed corpus for a domain but no domain expert who will commit. According to the launch rule, what is the correct read on this candidate?

- **It cannot launch as a vertical yet** ✅
- It can launch because the corpus sells
- It should launch once buyers appear
- It launches after the beachhead is chosen

**Explanation:** The launch rule is absolute: a vertical does not launch without a committed domain expert, because the expert twin is the product, and a vertical without one is just a corpus. So a rich corpus and no expert cannot launch as a vertical. Selling ease does not override this, so 'launch because the corpus sells' is wrong. Waiting for buyers does not supply an expert, so 'launch once buyers appear' is wrong. The beachhead is chosen only after a candidate survives the tests, so 'after the beachhead' skips the gate. This is why Ayesha's accounting vertical waited for her aunt to sign before anything launched.

### Q2 — Choosing Your Vertical (intro)

**Q:** The first-job test names four properties of a good first job for a Digital FTE. Which option best describes a job that fits it?

- A rare task done once, hard to measure
- **Repeats weekly, measurable, already reviewed, fixable** ✅
- A creative one-off nobody currently reviews, checks, or measures
- A one-way action nobody can undo

**Explanation:** The first-job test requires four properties: the work repeats daily or weekly, the output can be measured, a supervisor already reviews it today, and mistakes can be fixed. Only the repeats-measurable-reviewed-fixable option carries all four. A rare, unmeasurable task fails the repetition and measurement properties. A creative task with no supervisor fails the review property, so no contract of success can be checked. A one-way action nobody can undo fails the fixable property outright, which is exactly why regulated work defaults to preparation and recommendation. In real deployments, an audit working-paper review, done weekly and already checked by a reviewer, is a textbook fit.

### Q3 — Choosing Your Vertical (intro)

**Q:** The order rule says build first, sell second. What must exist before anything in the vertical business starts?

- A signed contract of success with a paying customer
- **One slice: one professional outcome, covered completely** ✅
- A finished trio, with twin and builder deployed
- A quoted license cost from every standards body

**Explanation:** The order rule states that nothing in the vertical business starts until one slice of the System of Record exists: one professional outcome, covered completely. A signed contract comes later, and it cannot come first because the buyer supplies the baseline only after he has been shown something. A finished trio is far beyond the slice; the twin and builder are built after all four gates pass and stand on the System of Record. Quoted license costs are gate 4 and gate 2 material, not the thing that opens the demand side. The reasoning is plain: a graduate with nothing governed has nothing to bring to a buyer, and a buyer shown nothing will not name his own starting number.

### Q4 — Choosing Your Vertical (intro)

**Q:** The page says a wrong vertical costs a year, and gives a sharper reason than lost market opportunity. What is it?

- Regulators will not approve a second attempt
- The expert's licence cannot be transferred
- **It is a year spent on the one asset she was going to own** ✅
- The shared Agent Factory System of Record has to be rebuilt

**Explanation:** A vendor-neutral FDE carries two Systems of Record: the shared Agent Factory System of Record, which the ecosystem gives her and which is identical at every client, and the profession, which she builds herself. Only the second is hers, so a wrong vertical is a year spent on the one asset she was going to own, with nothing to show at the end. Regulators have no say in whether a graduate tries a second vertical. The expert's licence is a partnership matter and not the reason the year hurts. The shared one never needs rebuilding, because it belongs to the ecosystem and travels with her unchanged. This is why the page says choose the vertical very carefully and the beachhead quickly.

### Q5 — Choosing Your Vertical (intro)

**Q:** A team is unsure whether to spend weeks perfecting the beachhead or the vertical choice. What does the page's cost rule advise?

- Perfect the beachhead first; the vertical stays reversible
- Both choices cost the same to reverse
- **Choose the vertical carefully; pick the beachhead quickly** ✅
- Delay both until buyers are confirmed

**Explanation:** A wrong beachhead costs three months; a wrong vertical costs a year. You can change a beachhead because everything stays inside the same vertical, so the vertical is what you choose slowly and carefully, and the beachhead is what you pick quickly. Perfecting the beachhead first inverts the risk, since the beachhead is the cheap, reversible choice. The two do not cost the same to reverse, which is the whole point of the rule. Delaying both wastes the time the fast beachhead decision is meant to save. A founder who agonizes over which audit segment to start, while never committing to accounting itself, has the priorities backwards.

### Q6 — Step 1: Name one body of professional work, not an industry

**Q:** Someone proposes 'healthcare' as their vertical. Applying Step 1, what should they be told?

- Healthcare is narrow enough to build
- Healthcare is a fine vertical to start
- Add more services to widen it
- **It is an industry; cut it smaller** ✅

**Explanation:** Step 1 says an industry like healthcare or banking is not a vertical, because it contains many kinds of work and no single expert could stand behind it. The correct move is to cut it smaller, toward one body of professional work. Calling healthcare 'narrow enough' or 'a fine vertical' ignores that a System of Record for a whole industry would be a library, not a corpus. Widening it with more services moves the wrong direction entirely. The page's own example cuts healthcare to revenue-cycle management, then to medical coding review, which is a body of work one person's career could cover.

### Q7 — Step 1: Name one body of professional work, not an industry

**Q:** What is the simple test from Step 1 for whether a body of work is narrow enough to be a vertical?

- Whether many different kinds of people do it
- **Whether one person's whole career is this work** ✅
- Whether the market is large enough
- Whether it spans several different professions at once

**Explanation:** Step 1 gives one test: can you imagine one person whose whole career is this work? If yes, it is narrow enough. If the honest answer is 'several different kinds of people,' you have named an industry and must cut it smaller before continuing. Market size is a Step 2 and Test 5 concern, not the narrowness test, so a large market never makes an industry narrow enough. Spanning several professions at once is the failure signal, not the goal, since no single expert could stand behind that much work. This is why 'corporate credit' or 'tax practice' passes: each names a career one person can hold, whereas 'banking' names an industry many different careers live inside.

- A vertical must always be an entire profession
- Industries make the best verticals

### Q9 — Step 2: Screen it for selling ease

**Q:** A candidate domain has no single number both sides can measure to define success before work starts. On the screen, what does this predict?

- **Every sale will be slow** ✅
- The sale will be fast
- Guardrail numbers become unnecessary
- The expert becomes optional

**Explanation:** Screen question 1 asks whether you can define success with one main number that both sides can measure, plus guardrail numbers. If you cannot describe success this way in a few sentences, the page says every sale will be slow. A fast sale is the opposite of what a missing success number predicts. Guardrail numbers become more important, not unnecessary, since speed that creates errors is not success. The expert is required by the launch rule regardless, so measurement never makes the expert optional. In practice, a buyer who cannot agree on 'hours per file' with you will stall on every later step.

### Q10 — Step 2: Screen it for selling ease

**Q:** Two domains are identical except one asks buyers for a brand-new budget and one redirects money already spent. Which scores higher on screen question 2, and why?

- The new-budget one; it clearly signals bold ambition
- Neither; budgets do not matter here
- **The existing-spend one; fewer meetings needed** ✅
- The new-budget one; bigger deals await

**Explanation:** Screen question 2 asks whether a buyer already spends money on the problem. You want to redirect existing spending, because a new budget needs many meetings while existing spending usually needs far fewer. So the existing-spend domain scores higher. Ambition and 'bigger deals' do not offset the meetings a new budget demands, so both new-budget answers are wrong. Budgets very much matter here, which is the entire question, so 'neither' is wrong. This is why a vertical that slots into money a firm already pays a bookkeeping outsourcer, for example, sells faster than one asking finance to create a line item.

### Q11 — Step 2: Screen it for selling ease

**Q:** A domain's knowledge lives in a private platform's policies that change monthly with no version history. On screen question 4, how should it score?

- High, because policies are public and free
- High; the platform keeps it current
- Middle; a written license fixes it
- **Low; a governed corpus cannot stay current** ✅

**Explanation:** Screen question 4 asks whether you can get the knowledge legally and keep it governed. Private platform policies that change every month with no version history are the hardest case, because you cannot keep a governed corpus current when its owner rewrites the rules without notice. So it scores low. The policies are not public laws, so 'public and free' is false. The platform changing rules without notice is the problem, not a benefit, so 'keeps it current' is wrong. A written license does not fix an owner who keeps no version history, so 'a license fixes it' misses the governance failure. This is exactly why e-commerce operations parks as a service domain.

### Q12 — Step 2: Screen it for selling ease

**Q:** A founder scores a candidate at 5.5 on the six-question screen average. Under the rule, what happens next?

- **Below 6, so take an honest exit** ✅
- Above 6, so continue to the tests
- Exactly at the launch threshold
- Re-score until it clears 6

**Explanation:** The screen rule is: score 6 out of 10 or higher, continue; below 6, take an exit. A 5.5 is below 6, so the candidate takes one of the two honest exits, service domain or parked. It is not above 6, so continuing to the tests is wrong. The threshold is 6, and 5.5 sits below it, so it is not exactly at the threshold. Re-scoring until it clears 6 is manipulating the number rather than reading it, and the anchors demand a sentence of evidence beside every score. A candidate that lands at 5.5 with honest evidence is telling you to serve it or park it, not to build a trio.

- Park it with a written condition
- Launch it as a full vertical

### Q14 — Step 2: Screen it for selling ease

**Q:** A graduate takes the service-domain exit. Does the order rule require her to build a slice first?

- **No; the service ladder starts with a client and needs no slice** ✅
- Yes; every kind of paid work requires a slice first
- Yes, but a smaller slice is acceptable there

A service domain needs no slice because it sells no vertical: you build the client's own governed source, or manufacture Workers with the deployed generic tools, and the client pays for that work. So the order rule does not reach across to this exit, which rules out both 'yes' answers, and there is no such thing as a smaller slice, since thin already means one outcome covered completely. But service work is not ungoverned: a client's System of Record still needs an owner, versions, review, and access control, so 'no governed content at all' is wrong. ### Q15 — Test 1: Corpus shape

- Buyers, their budgets, and purchasing timelines listed
- Experts, sponsors, and their signed contracts
- **A cited corpus, a rules map, reflexes** ✅
- Websites, MCP servers, and the pgvector index

**Explanation:** Test 1 (corpus shape) asks whether knowledge fits three forms: a corpus the agent cites, a map of rules that is always loaded, and reflexes, the step-by-step procedures. Buyers, budgets, and timelines are Test 5 market concerns, not knowledge forms. Experts, sponsors, and contracts belong to Test 3 and the validation gates, not corpus shape. Websites, MCP servers, and pgvector are the delivery technologies from the reference implementation, not the forms knowledge takes. The evidence Test 1 wants makes the three forms concrete: name ten key sources, three unbreakable rules, and three complete procedures. If you cannot, the knowledge may be too thin or never written down.

- It disqualifies the whole vertical instantly
- **It can be captured, but that adds months** ✅

### Q24 — Test 4: Governing rules

**Q:** Test 4 notes that each jurisdiction is a separate build. What opportunity does the page draw from this?

- A single build automatically covers all the countries
- Foreign rules make a domain unbuildable
- Jurisdictions should be merged into one

### Q26 — Test 5: Market and economics

**Q:** Why does the page call an invented money number worse than an empty space?

- **It fixes thinking on a baseless figure** ✅
- It will always overstate the real market
- It violates a named regulator's rule

Bragging about the newest technology says nothing about the profession's judgment, which is the real differentiator. The page also warns to test the sentence honestly: if it only sounds good in your own meetings, the test is not passed. ### Q33 — Step 4: Choose the beachhead, and let the expert decide

**Q:** Step 4 says do not build everything at once. Which three qualities define the beachhead you should choose?

- Biggest market, the most buyers, highest price
- Most regulators, hardest rules, slowest sale
- Newest technology, most competitors, and the lowest possible cost
- **Clearest knowledge, checkable Worker, shortest contract path** ✅

**Explanation:** Step 4 defines the beachhead as the segment with the clearest knowledge, the most checkable first Worker, and the shortest path to a signed contract of success.
