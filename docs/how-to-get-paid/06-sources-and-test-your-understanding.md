### Q1.

**Q:** What are the two problems this page says exist at the same time, and what does it call the space between them?

- Models remain too weak for real production work, so the space between them is a research problem
- **Developers cannot find work while companies cannot make AI pay, so the space between is a deployment gap** ✅
- There are far too many junior developers and too few models, so the space between them is a training problem
- Regulation moves slowly while adoption moves quickly, so the space between them is a policy gap

**Explanation:** Agents write a growing share of routine code while junior roles thin and freelance boards crowd, so developers cannot find work. At the same time about 95 percent of custom enterprise pilots show no measurable financial return, so companies cannot make AI pay. The page names the distance between them a deployment gap exactly the width of one job.

### Q2.

**Q:** The page insists the 95 percent figure is usually quoted carelessly. What does it actually say?

- That the software never actually ran in production at any of the companies studied
- That the models fell well short of their published benchmark scores in real use
- **That the money did not show up, rather than that the models themselves failed** ✅
- That the pilots were abandoned long before any working code had been written

**Explanation:** The number does not say the models failed or that the software never ran. It says the money did not show up, and the cause points at fit: a model is a general capability while a company needs a specific outcome. A pilot with an impressive demo and no measurable return is almost always one that stopped at the demo — a services problem, not a technology problem.

### Q3.

**Q:** What does a Forward Deployed Engineer do differently from a normal software engineer?

- **They go inside the customer's company and build there, on real data, until value lands** ✅
- They specialise in one model provider and become deeply certified in that vendor's platform
- They build the product at headquarters and hand it to a customer success team
- They sell the software licences and then supervise the customer's own engineers as they build it

**Explanation:** The role was invented at Palantir and does the opposite of the normal job. Instead of building a product at headquarters, the FDE goes inside the customer's company, sits with the people doing the work, and builds the solution there, on real data, until real value lands.

*(Source: Section: The market's answer: the Forward Deployed Engineer)*

### Q4.

**Q:** What are the two gates the market keeps shut, and what single cause does the page give for both?

- Cost and complexity, both caused by the high price of frontier models today
- **Lock-in and the hiring door, both caused by the vendor's platform being the thing deployed** ✅
- Language and time zones, both caused by where the paying customers happen to be
- Certification and security clearance, both caused by enterprise procurement rules

**Explanation:** The first gate is lock-in and it hurts the client: the vendor's engineer wires one platform into everything and leaves. The second is the hiring door and it hurts you: the role lives on the vendor's payroll and is mostly senior. Both have one cause — the vendor's platform is the thing being deployed and the vendor decides who deploys it.

*(Source: Section: The two gates the market keeps shut)*

### Q5.

**Q:** Each word in 'vendor-neutral vertical FDE' solves one problem. Which solves which?

**Explanation:** Vendor-neutral solves lock-in: bound to no platform, your client switches when a better model or cheaper runtime arrives. Vertical solves the empty-hands problem: take the vendor's platform away and specialization has to land somewhere — it lands on the profession.

### Q10.

**Q:** Station 5 gives a solo builder two pieces of advice on top of the general method. What are they?
